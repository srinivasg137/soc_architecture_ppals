Wider datapath or higher frequency?

Three ways to increase bandwidth. One system that must supply the data, meet timing and stay within budget.

The engineering question

A 512 bit datapath at 1 GHz must deliver twice its useful throughput. Should we widen it to 1024 bits, raise the clock to 2 GHz, or use two engines?

First check where bandwidth is lost. An engine waiting for memory needs a different solution from one moving partially filled words. Consider an internal DMA payload path between a PCIe subsystem and the SoC fabric. Both ends must support the target.

The operating points are illustrative design assumptions, not vendor measurements or PCIe link rates.

Peak capacity is only the beginning

ArchitecturePeak capacity
512 bits at 1 GHz64 GB/s
1024 bits at 1 GHz128 GB/s
512 bits at 2 GHz128 GB/s
Two 512 bit engines at 1 GHz128 GB/s aggregate

These one direction rates assume one accepted word per cycle. If the original sustains 48 GB/s, the target is 96 GB/s. A useful payload target of 128 GB/s leaves no room for overhead in a nominal 128 GB/s design.

Useful throughput = (width ÷ 8) × frequency × transfer utilization × useful byte occupancy

For example, 128 GB/s × 0.85 × 0.90 gives 97.92 GB/s, only 2% above the target. For two engines, calculate their rates separately and add them.

When widening helps

A wider engine suits long payloads when the processing lanes, buffers and fabric can move more data. An unchanged 512 bit port at 1 GHz still limits that path to 64 GB/s. A width converter does not create bandwidth.

Suppose each data item contains 48 bytes and the engine accepts one item per beat, without packing. At 1 GHz, both a 512 bit design and a 1024 bit design deliver 48 GB/s. Widening helps this workload only if the design can pack and process multiple items together.

Inspect size and alignment distributions. A FIFO with 64 entries stores 4 KiB at 512 bits and 8 KiB at 1024 bits, before metadata. Register load, muxes, control fanout and routing also change.

What changes at 2 GHz

The period falls from 1 ns to 0.5 ns. Clock to Q, setup, uncertainty and skew leave less time for logic and wires. With an illustrative 100 ps allowance, the remaining budget falls from 900 ps to 400 ps.

Pipelining can shorten a logic path, but data, tags, byte enables and errors must remain aligned. Feedback may still limit how often new work enters. Memory macros, crossings and the downstream fabric must support the higher service rate.

Eight stages at 1 GHz and sixteen at 2 GHz both give 8 ns of nominal pipeline latency. Higher throughput does not necessarily mean a shorter completion time for one operation.

What two engines require

Replication retains the local clock target but adds control state, dispatch and possibly result ordering. Independent descriptors or flows are natural candidates. A single dependency chain cannot keep both engines busy without another mechanism.

Both engines need enough aggregate input and output bandwidth. Shared SRAM ports, memory banks and merge logic can become bottlenecks. If one engine sustains 48 GB/s and two sustain 80 GB/s, scaling efficiency is 80 ÷ 96 = 83.3%. The pair still misses the target.

Inspect each engine separately. An average utilization can hide uneven work distribution or source starvation.

Compare energy and physical cost

Dynamic power ≈ activity × capacitance × voltage² × frequency

Doubling frequency doubles this component only when everything else stays constant. If voltage rises from an assumed 0.75 V to 0.90 V, the factor becomes 2 × (0.90 ÷ 0.75)² = 2.88. That is a conditional example, not a process requirement.

Widening or replication also changes switched capacitance. Compare energy per useful byte over the same workload, including clock power, leakage, buffering and crossings. For bursty traffic, include idle time and wakeup overhead.

If wires dominate, examine locality and placement. Wider buses increase connectivity demand; faster clocks give long routes less time. Two local engines help only when their data and buffers can remain local.

Make a provisional architecture decision

Without physical trials, choose a candidate with explicit conditions. Record the target throughput and latency, workload traces, memory service, clock assumptions, macro limits, expected placement and power accounting boundary.

  • Packed payloads and matching wide interfaces: investigate 1024 bits at 1 GHz.
  • Small items with credible clock and memory evidence: investigate 512 bits at 2 GHz.
  • Independent queues and separate banks: investigate two 512 bit engines.
  • Shared memory is saturated: improve service or data reuse first.

Give each unresolved assumption an owner and a validation milestone. Keep a fallback until timing, power and routing evidence support the choice.
